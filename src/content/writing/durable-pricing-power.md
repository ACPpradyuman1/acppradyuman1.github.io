<span class="lede">The opening claim</span> is simple: pricing power is the single best predictor of long-run returns, and most investors underprice how rare it actually is. This piece lays out how to test for it, and where the test usually fails.

Most businesses can raise prices once. Few can do it every year without losing volume. The difference between the two is almost never visible in a single income statement — it shows up over a full cycle, when a competitor undercuts and customers either leave or don't.

## What actually signals pricing power

Gross margin stability through a cost shock is the cleanest tell. A business that holds margin while input costs rise is passing the increase through — and the customer is staying.

> The market usually re-rates pricing power a year after it's already visible in the numbers.

Emphasis matters here: this isn't about *market share*, it's about **retained pricing** after a shock. Watch names like <code>NVDA</code> or <code>COST</code> for the pattern — pricing held while volume held.

## where this breaks

Every thesis needs a section for the case against it.

<div class="risk-list">
  <div class="item">
    <span class="marker">01</span>
    <span>A single large customer with real bargaining power can force price concessions long before it shows up in aggregate margin data.</span>
  </div>
  <div class="item">
    <span class="marker">02</span>
    <span>Regulatory attention on pricing tends to arrive right after the market has fully priced the thesis in.</span>
  </div>
  <div class="item">
    <span class="marker">03</span>
    <span>Substitution risk is invisible until a new entrant makes it visible all at once.</span>
  </div>
</div>

The closing point is the same as the opening one: pricing power is a behavior observed under pressure, not a line item. Wait for the next cost shock before deciding whether a business actually has it.
