<span class="lede">The gap</span> Roughly one in five semiconductor design engineers in the world sits in India. Almost none of the intellectual property they produce is owned here. The work gets done in Bengaluru and Hyderabad, and the value accrues to companies headquartered somewhere else.

That arrangement has held for about two decades, and it has been a good business. Services revenue is predictable, margins are respectable, and the risk sits with whoever owns the product. What it does not do is compound. A design services firm does not accumulate IP, does not capture the upside when a chip sells a hundred million units, and does not build a position anyone has to reckon with.

The interesting question is whether that is changing, and if so, who is positioned to benefit. HrdWyr is the company that prompted me to work through it.

## why the market is moving now

Four numbers frame the shift.

| Signal | Figure | What it indicates |
|---|---|---|
| Semiconductor rally since ChatGPT's launch | 700%+ | Chips have been repriced as AI infrastructure. Every other major sector sits under 200% over the same period. |
| Share of the world's most advanced chips made in Taiwan | 90%+ | Extreme geographic concentration. A conflict scenario has been modelled at around $10 trillion in economic damage. |
| India's semiconductor market, 2026 to 2031 | $62 Bn to $155 Bn | Implies a move from roughly 5 to 6% of global share to 9 to 10%. |
| Share of global semiconductor design engineers already in India | ~20% | The talent base exists. It has existed for years. |

The first two explain why capital and policy attention arrived. The second two explain why India specifically. Taken together they describe a market where the strategic case and the supply of people point in the same direction for the first time.

## the strategic choice india made

India is not trying to compete on leading-edge fabrication, and that decision is the most sensible thing about the strategy. Advanced fabs are among the most capital-intensive assets in the industrial economy, with payback horizons measured in decades and a competitive field that has already consolidated to a handful of players.

The chosen segments are different in character.

| Segment | Capital intensity | Time to revenue | India's position |
|---|---|---|---|
| Advanced fabrication | Extreme | Decade plus | Not contested, correctly |
| Chip design | Low | Short | Deep existing talent base, mature ecosystem |
| Packaging and assembly (ATMP) | Moderate | Medium | Building, with Tata Electronics as an early anchor |
| Edge and low-power silicon | Low to moderate | Short to medium | Open, with fewer entrenched incumbents |

The India Semiconductor Mission supports this with design-linked incentives covering up to 50% of design costs. That is a meaningful subsidy on the exact input where India already has an advantage, which is rarer in industrial policy than it should be.

The strategy, in short, is to enter where capital requirements are lowest and the existing talent is most relevant, and to build from there rather than attempting to leapfrog.

## the company

HrdWyr is a fabless semiconductor company in Bengaluru, founded in 2023.

| | |
|---|---|
| **What they build** | Chips with AI designed into the architecture rather than added on afterwards, so a device can process data locally instead of sending it to a data centre. First product line is Indus, ultra-low-power intelligent edge silicon. |
| **Target applications** | Consumer devices, automotive, white goods, data centres |
| **Business model** | Fabless. They design the chip, a foundry manufactures it, an assembly partner packages and tests it, and chips are sold to device makers per unit. |
| **Revenue timing** | Revenue follows a design win by 12 to 24 months |
| **Funding** | $13 Mn Series A, May 2026, led by Ideaspring Capital with Singularity AMC, Avatar Growth Capital and Persistent Systems |
| **First proof point** | Indus 1011, announced August 2025 with boAt and Tata Electronics |

The Indus 1011 is worth dwelling on. It is a battery management chip designed to boAt's requirements, with Tata Electronics handling assembly, packaging and testing domestically, destined for premium TWS charging cases. Designed in India, packaged in India. That is not a sentence that has been available very often, and it is the closest thing the company has to evidence that the thesis works in practice rather than on paper.

The business model deserves one note of caution. Fabless is the right structure for a company entering this market, because the capital burden of fabrication is pushed onto the foundry. It also means HrdWyr does not control manufacturing capacity, cost, or timing, and is exposed to foundry allocation decisions it does not make.

They met at NexGen Power Systems in 2022 and founded HrdWyr the following year. One detail is worth noting for what it says about the network: one of Sivakumar's early Intel Capital India investments was Persistent Systems, which is an investor in HrdWyr today.

What this team represents is unusual. Between them they spent sixty years building the capability that made India the world's semiconductor back office. They are now betting against the logic of their own careers, which is either conviction or a very expensive way to prove a point.

## the hypothesis

My working view is that the question here is conversion, not capability.

The capability argument has effectively been settled. India has designed complex silicon for twenty years, at scale, for the most demanding customers in the industry. Nobody seriously disputes that the engineering can be done.

Conversion is a different problem and a harder one. Owning product IP requires holding customer relationships, absorbing inventory and demand risk, funding development ahead of revenue, and surviving the gap between a design win and the money that follows it. Those are commercial and financial muscles, not engineering ones, and the services model specifically did not build them.

So the interesting thing about HrdWyr is not whether they can design a good chip. It is whether a team that spent its career on the services side of that line can operate on the product side of it.

## what i would want to validate

| Question | Why it matters | What would change my view |
|---|---|---|
| Is boAt actually shipping with the Indus 1011, or is it still exploratory? | The entire proof point rests on this. An announced partnership and a shipping product are different things. | Confirmed volume shipping would move this from an interesting story to a real commercial position. |
| What is the $13 Mn meant to buy? Working chip, or revenue? | Determines whether the next raise happens from strength or necessity, and on whose terms. | A clear path to revenue inside this raise would materially de-risk the next one. |
| Which customer segment comes first? | Consumer, EV, white goods and data centres have been mentioned. These have very different sales cycles, margins and capital needs. | A single named beachhead segment with a pipeline behind it would signal commercial discipline. |
| Where does the IP actually sit, and how is the team split? | Both founders are US-based while the company builds in India. The location of the IP affects valuation, acquirer set, and the India-ownership thesis itself. | IP held in India with a substantive India team would support the thesis. The reverse would undercut the premise. |

That last question is the one I would ask first. The entire argument for this company as an India IP story depends on the answer, and it is the one the public material does not settle.

## what would break this

    <span><strong>The revenue lag.</strong> Twelve to twenty-four months from design win to revenue means the company will need to raise again before the first product line proves itself economically. That timing is normal for semiconductors and brutal for a company without a long track record, because the second raise gets priced on promises rather than numbers.</span>
  </div>

    <span class="marker">03</span>

Tata Electronics doing ATMP domestically is genuinely new, and new supply chains have failure modes that mature ones have already designed out. That risk sits outside HrdWyr's control entirely.</span>

None of these are disqualifying. All three are things I would want priced into whatever the next round looks like.

## where i land

I am not at a conviction call, and I would be suspicious of anyone who reached one from public information on a company this young.

What I will say is that the setup is more interesting than most India deep tech I have looked at. The macro case is real rather than narrative, the strategic positioning avoids the trap of competing where India cannot win, the founders have credibility that is difficult to manufacture, and there is at least one product with named partners attached to it rather than a roadmap.

The thing I would watch is the boAt relationship converting into shipped volume, and a second named customer appearing. If both happen in the next four quarters, the conversion argument starts to look answered. If neither does, this is a very good team with a very good story, which is a category that has historically not been short of examples.
